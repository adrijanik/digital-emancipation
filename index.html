Digital EmancipationProperty, Banks, and the Completed Web

A proposal for giving people title to their data, a place to keep it, and a way to be paid for it. The standards are mostly there. What is missing is distribution, and three primitives.

Status. A vision paper and a proposal, not research results, a product or advice. The demo is a simulation with invented people and prices. Read the disclaimers before relying on anything here.

Read the paper (Springer) · Conference site · How to cite

The idea in five sentences

  1. Platforms hold the data people generate, and people cannot leave with it. That is a holdup problem, and the result is a kind of digital serfdom.
  2. The standards to fix this largely exist: identity, credentials, access control, provenance, policy and query.
  3. What is missing is distribution, and three primitives: a Title that binds terms to a claim, a Settlement layer that puts a price on a query, and a Negotiation protocol for agents.
  4. A bank is the practical first carrier, because it already checks identity, has branches and recovery, and can be supervised. It is the first line, not the only one: any supervised custodian can carry an account. Ownership stays in neutral standards, so the carrier is a tenant and not a landlord.
  5. A Data Account Directive, modelled on the Payment Accounts Directive, would make such an account a right.

It can be falsified. A pilot with one bank and one high-value cohort would show whether revenue can cover custody cost, and whether anyone ever switches carrier.

Where it is weakest, and where help is wanted

  • Relational data. My data reveals yours. The paper's answer is cohort-level title and regulating harmful inference. That is a direction, not a design.
  • The decentralised objection. Why banks and not keys and wallets? The argument is recovery and reach, but it deserves its strongest critics.
  • The economics. One person's data may be worth cents. The paper's figures are estimates, not measurements.
  • Proving training use. Nobody can yet show from outside that a model trained on a given record. Canary records give evidence, not proof.
  • Building the primitives. Title, Settlement and Negotiation are sketched here. They need people who build standards.
  • A pilot. One bank, one cohort. If you can host one, say so.

Disclaimers, and how much to trust each claim

This is a proposal offered for debate. Different parts of it deserve different levels of trust, so here they are separated.

Checked against public sourcesThat GDPR Article 20 gives a portability right, that the Payment Accounts Directive (2014/92/EU) gives a right to a basic payment account, and that the standards named here exist. Some are W3C Recommendations (RDF, SPARQL, SHACL, PROV-O, ODRL, DID, Verifiable Credentials). Others are community, industry or draft specifications: Solid, WebID, UMA 2.0, Open Payments and W3C Linked Web Storage, whose first public working draft appeared in March 2026. Specifications change, so check the current versions.
Argument and analogyThe serfdom and holdup framing, the land-reform and property-title analogy, the post-1929 regulatory analogy and the claim that a bank is the best first carrier. These are the author's arguments, offered as analogy and not as evidence.
EstimatesAny figure about what data might be worth, what custody costs, or what a bank might earn is the author's own estimate and has not been measured. The proposed pilot is meant to test them.
Illustrative onlyEverything in the demo and diagram: people, institutions, prices, hospital and platform names, credentials, tokens, ODRL offers, remedy clauses and receipts. Message formats are simplified, and some vocabulary is invented for the story. The SPARQL Anything queries are examples and have not been run against real data. The hashes are real SHA-256 computations over fictional records.
Read from public sources, not auditedDescriptions of earlier efforts (NatWest's 2020 Solid demonstration, Flanders' Athumi utility, Japan's information-bank scheme, Estonia's X-Road) and of the AI-crawling standards work (IETF aipref, RSL, Cloudflare pay-per-crawl, TDMRep). Some details were recalled from memory. They may be incomplete or out of date, so check them before citing.

Not advice. Nothing here is legal, financial, investment or regulatory advice. A Data Account Directive does not exist: it is a proposal for what a directive could say.
