The digital serf

You wrote ten thousand messages. You cannot take them with you.

The medieval serf was not a slave. He was bound in one respect only: he did not own the land he worked, and could not leave. Economists call it holdup.

The user of the platform Web in 2026 stands in precisely this relation to providers. The resemblance is structural, not rhetorical.

One day in the emancipated Web

Anna is a nurse in Lyon. It should not take wealth or technical skill for her to own her data.

While she sleeps

A pharmacology consortium queries her anonymised cohort and pays on arrival, into her current account.

Her pod

She holds the title. A bank hosts it, and competes for her custom. Leaving costs an afternoon.

At the clinic

A live, revocable view into the patient’s own pod. Revoking it actually revokes it.

In the evening

A model company offers terms for her photographs. The offer is low. She declines, and nobody remarks on it.

Freedom, delivered as infrastructure, is invisible; that is how you know it is finished.

The first carrier: a bank

Digital freedom is currently a luxury good.

A liberty that requires expertise is not liberty. It is aristocracy with extra steps.

Nearly every adult in the EU holds a bank account. Almost none holds a key pair they could find in an emergency. Banks are the practical first line, not the only one: any supervised custodian can carry the account.

Emancipation is not a proclamation. It is a financial operation.

Four roles, kept separable

Identity: issuer, not owner

Her DID lives on her devices. The bank, having done KYC anyway, issues credentials: adult, resident, nurse.

Custody: a vault, not a lake

Encrypted client-side. The bank holds a recovery share, never the plaintext, and recovery works at a counter.

Ingress: enforcing portability

Authorise once; the bank exercises her GDPR Article 20 and EU Data Act rights for her.

Market: a profit motive

Priced queries settle into the account she is paid into. The bank earns a basis-point fee.

Ownership is written in neutral standards. The carrier, a bank first, is a tenant, not a landlord.

The Data Account Directive

Modelled on the Payment Accounts Directive, which gave every EU resident the right to a basic bank account.

1

A right to a basic data account

2

Mandated custodian switching, over open storage standards (Linked Web Storage, from Solid)

3

Escrowed-key standards that prohibit custodian access to plaintext

4

Supervision of pod custody as a fiduciary function

Standards without obligation remain demos. Obligation without computational standards remains paperwork.

Three missing primitives

Title

A signed assertion that a named principal holds enumerated rights in a described graph. Joins DID, PROV-O, ODRL and Data Integrity.

Settlement

Priced Query: a handshake around any SPARQL endpoint. An ODRL offer is countersigned and settled before results flow.

Negotiation

An open protocol for agents to agree contracts over ODRL terms, with SHACL shapes as the schema of agreement.

Query becomes what it always secretly was: an act between principals, now visible, priced, and consensual.

Objections, and what they teach

Non-rivalry

Land titles do not prevent trespass; they make it actionable. Usage attestation, not prevention, is the enforcement layer.

Marginal value

One person’s records may be worth cents to a model (our estimate, untested). Then titles must pool into cohorts.

Relational data

Sharing my data reveals yours. The strongest objection: cohort-level title, and regulating harmful inference.

Inalienability

What trades is a bounded, revocable licence, never the right itself. The Directive sets floors before markets.

Falsifiable: a staged pilot with one bank and one high-value cohort. If revenue cannot clear custody cost, the economic premise fails.
